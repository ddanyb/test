5 Signs Your Popcorn Ceiling May Contain Asbestos

Textured ceilings were standard in St. Louis homes for decades. Here's what to look for before you sand, scrape, or drill into one.

If your house was built or last renovated before the late 1980s, there's a real chance the bumpy "popcorn" texture on your ceilings contains asbestos. It was a common, cheap way to hide seams and add a little sound dampening, and it stayed in wide use in the St. Louis area well after other parts of the country had started phasing it out. That doesn't mean every textured ceiling is hazardous — but it does mean it's worth knowing what to look for before anyone starts sanding, scraping, or drilling into one.

1. Your home was built before 1990

Asbestos-containing texture products were manufactured and sold in the U.S. into the late 1980s, and installed material stayed on ceilings long after that. If your home (or the section of it with the ceiling in question) dates from that era and the ceiling has never been redone, it's a candidate worth checking.

2. The texture is undisturbed and original

A ceiling that's never been painted, patched, or resurfaced is more likely to be the original material — which is exactly what you'd want tested if you're planning any work. A ceiling that's been skim-coated or replaced more recently is less of a concern, though it's still worth asking a previous owner or contractor what was actually done, since asbestos texture is sometimes painted over rather than removed.

3. You're planning a renovation, not just living with it

Undisturbed asbestos-containing material sitting quietly on a ceiling is generally not considered an urgent hazard — the risk comes from disturbing it. If you're not planning any work, the more useful question is whether it's intact and not flaking. If you are planning a remodel, a light fixture swap, popcorn removal, or anything that involves sanding or scraping the ceiling, that's the point to find out what you're dealing with first.

4. The ceiling is damaged, flaking, or crumbling

Asbestos fibers become a concern when material is damaged and can release dust into the air. Cracking, flaking, water damage, or any spot where the texture is crumbling away from the surface is worth taking seriously, even if you weren't planning to touch it. Water-damaged popcorn ceiling is a common way we get called out — moisture problems and material integrity problems often show up together.

5. You genuinely don't know, and that's the point

You can't identify asbestos by looking at it. Texture, color, and age are reasons to be cautious, not a diagnosis. The only way to know for sure is to have a sample tested before any work begins — which is also the first thing that happens with any project we take on, regardless of what it turns out to be.

What to do next

If any of this sounds like your situation, the safest move is simple: don't sand it, scrape it, or drill into it until you know what it is. Reach out and we'll walk you through what testing looks like and what your options are from there.
